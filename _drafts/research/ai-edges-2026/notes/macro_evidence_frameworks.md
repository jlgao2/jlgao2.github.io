## Key Question 1: Firm-level and economy-level ROI — where is value real?

### Takeaway
Measured economy-wide productivity has accelerated (US nonfarm productivity ~2.1–2.4% annualized since 2020/2024 versus 1.5% in the 2007–19 cycle), and the acceleration is concentrated in the three most AI-exposed sectors (finance, professional/technical services, information), but official statistics do not isolate AI's contribution and firm surveys still show most companies cannot attribute EBIT impact to AI (McKinsey 2026: ~37% report any EBIT impact; ~6% "high performers"). The firm-level evidence is bimodal: a thin layer of quantified winners (banks, customer-support automation, coding) and a broad base of pilots with no measured return.

### Cited Findings

**Aggregate productivity (measured data)**
- BLS: nonfarm business labor productivity rose 1.4% (annualized) in Q2 2026 and 2.2% year-over-year; Q1 2026 revised to 0.8%. The annualized growth rate for the current business cycle (from Q4 2019) is 2.1% versus 1.5% for the 2007–2019 cycle. BLS states that it captures AI only implicitly through its software capital measure, so AI effects are not separately identified. — [BLS Productivity and Costs Q2 2026 revised](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/pdf/prod2.pdf); [BLS productivity home](https://www.bls.gov/productivity/)
- Dallas Fed (July 2026): US labor productivity has grown at a 2.4% annualized rate since the beginning of 2024; international comparison shows the pattern "appears related to high AI use." A secondary write-up of the same work: finance, professional/technical services and information account for only 16% of hours worked but delivered 3.7% annualized productivity growth since early 2024. — [Dallas Fed, "International comparisons show AI effect on productivity"](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0707); secondary sector breakdown via [Dataporium](https://dataporium.tech/articles/productivity-growth-and-ai-in-2026-what-the-bls-data-shows-so-far)
- Atlanta Fed working paper (March 2026, executive survey): AI-driven labor productivity gains are positive, vary across sectors, and are expected to strengthen in 2026; gains are "not primarily driven by firms' capital deepening"; firm-size-and-sector-weighted aggregate employment expected to decline by less than 0.4% due to AI in 2026. The NBER version notes that firm-reported productivity growth (mean 3.0% for 2026) far exceeds the implied measure (1.8%). — [Atlanta Fed WP 2026-04](https://www.atlantafed.org/-/media/Project/Atlanta/FRBA/Documents/research/publication/working-paper/2026/03/25/04-artificial-intelligence-productivity-and-the-workforce-evidence-from-corporate-executives.pdf); [NBER w34984](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34984/w34984.pdf)

### Inferences
- The aggregate productivity pickup since 2024 is real and sector-concentrated in exactly the places where AI usage is highest, which is consistent with (but does not prove) an AI contribution. The Atlanta Fed finding that gains are not from capital deepening points to AI-as-labor-augmentation rather than the capex itself.
- The gap between firm-reported gains (3.0%) and implied measures (1.8%) in the Atlanta Fed/NBER survey, and the McKinsey 37% attribution rate, suggest substantial "AI-washing" in self-reported ROI.
- Value is real where tasks are high-volume, text/code-heavy and measurable (software engineering, customer support, document processing, back-office finance); broad enterprise transformation remains mostly unmeasured.

## Key Question 2: Labor — which jobs and sectors show measured effects?

### Takeaway
The best-identified labor effect is at the entry level in AI-exposed occupations (Stanford/ADP: 22–25-year-olds in the most exposed jobs ~19% below less-exposed peers in the Aug 2026 update, driven by fewer hires not layoffs), while the Yale Budget Lab continues to find no aggregate disruption through August 2026 CPS data. Employer-stated AI layoff attributions spiked (Challenger: 40% of cuts in May 2026) then fell sharply (9% monthly share by September), consistent with partial "AI-washing."

- Stanford Digital Economy Lab, "Canaries in the Coal Mine?" (original Aug 2025; update Aug 2026): using ADP payroll data, employment for 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations is ~19% below less-exposed peers in the 2026 update (prior release: 13% per one outlet, 15% per others); decline comes mainly from fewer new hires rather than layoffs; older workers show no comparable gap; authors describe results as descriptive rather than causal. ### Inferences
- Measured effects are concentrated in (a) young workers in exposed occupations (software, customer service, some clerical) and (b) customer-support headcount at firms that deployed agents. Aggregate unemployment effects remain undetectable, consistent with Waller's "hiring freeze first" channel.
- The spike-then-fade in Challenger AI attributions (40% in May to 9% in Sept) argues that a chunk of H1 2026 attributions were narrative-driven rather than technology-driven.

## Key Question 3: Value capture — who has actually made money so far?

### Takeaway
Through October 2026 the money has overwhelmingly been captured by the picks-and-shovels layer (Nvidia: $89.0B data-center revenue in a single quarter at 75% gross margin) and, in revenue if not profit, by two model labs (Anthropic ~$65B run-rate in July 2026; OpenAI ~$40–50B, burning cash with ~$665–856B of compute commitments). The application layer has a handful of breakout companies (Cursor ~$4B ARR) but in aggregate is one to two orders of magnitude smaller than infrastructure spend; Sequoia's Cahn now puts the required revenue at ~$3T against ~$1.5T of 2026 infrastructure spend.

## Key Question 4: Theory of edges — which business models capture AI value durably, and what has been confirmed or falsified since 2023–24?

### Takeaway
The live 2026 debate has moved from "models are a commodity, apps win" (the dominant 2023–24 VC view) to evidence that integrated frontier labs and scale aggregators are capturing more than expected (Thompson's "Frontier Overhangs," "Aggregators and AI"), while per-seat SaaS was repriced hard in Feb 2026 ("SaaSpocalypse," ~$285B erased in 48 hours after Claude Cowork). Jevons-style effects are visible in inference (per-token prices at fixed capability falling ~10x/yr while total spend rises), supporting the "AI as deflation of expertise" frame where gains accrue to owners of distribution, proprietary data, and regulated/relationship moats rather than to the deflating input.

### Cited Findings

**Ben Thompson / Stratechery (2026; paywalled, from search excerpts)**
- "Frontier Overhangs" (2026): profits flow away from modular, commoditized parts of the value chain toward integrated, differentiated parts; concludes "Anthropic and OpenAI are poised to be more profitable than seemed likely late last year." — [Stratechery, Frontier Overhangs](https://stratechery.com/2026/frontier-overhangs/)
- "Winners, Losers, and the Unknown" (2026): "the most recent evidence, particularly Copilot Cowork, is that the companies best able to harness model capabilities are the model makers themselves." — [Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/2026/winners-losers-and-the-unknown/)
- "Aggregators and AI" (2026): monolithic network-effects companies (aggregators) are poised to be big winners from AI. — [Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/2026/aggregators-and-ai/)
- "The CapEx Train Keeps Rolling" (2026): "if AI is as valuable as it seems, then it should pay for itself, but that hasn't happened yet." — [Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/2026/the-capex-train-keeps-rolling/)
- "Agents Over Bubbles" (March 2026): "I don't think we're in a bubble," which he calls "possibly the truest evidence that we are." — [Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/2026/agents-over-bubbles/)

### Inferences
- The durable-edge framework that fits the 2026 evidence: value flows to (a) scarce physical complements (advanced compute, memory, power, land), (b) integrated frontier labs that own both model and product surface, (c) aggregators with demand-side scale, and (d) end users with proprietary data and regulated/relationship moats who buy intelligence as a deflating input. It flows away from modular middle layers (mid-tier models, per-seat point solutions, wrapper apps).
- "AI as deflation of expertise" is supported by the token-price data: the input price collapses ~10x/yr, so the supplier of the input struggles to hold margin unless at the frontier, and the gains accrue to whoever has demand aggregation or a non-AI moat.

## Key Question 6: Historical analogues and what each implies for who makes money across the cycle

### Takeaway
The 2026 debate uses three analogues — railways (1840s UK / 1870s–90s US), 1999–2002 telecom/fiber, and the electricity dynamo lag — and the consensus among those invoking them is that the technology endures while early infrastructure equity holders are frequently wiped out, with the durable winners being users of the cheapened input (as with dark fiber enabling the 2000s web). The main "this time is different" rebuttal is that today's hyperscalers are self-funding profit machines unlikely to default, though 2026's shift to debt financing erodes that argument.

### Cited Findings
- Derek Thompson (Oct/Nov 2025, interview with Kedrosky): the railroad era "didn't make railroads a bad idea, but the carnage along the way was dramatic"; Kedrosky calls AI one of the five largest capex bubbles in history. — [Derek Thompson, "Yes, AI Is a Bubble"](https://www.derekthompson.org/p/yes-ai-is-a-bubble-there-is-no-question)

- Cisco-vs-Amazon mapping: Nvidia is today's Cisco (equipment seller at peak margin); the hyperscalers are simultaneously the carriers (asset owners with debt) and candidate Amazons (demand aggregators). The 2026 shift to bond/SPV/private-credit funding makes the hyperscalers look more carrier-like than in 2024.
- The fiber lesson is that the overbuild's cheap bandwidth went to users (Google, Amazon, Netflix). The AI analogue is that if compute is overbuilt, token prices fall further and the beneficiaries are the application layer and enterprise buyers in Q7, not the GPU owners.
- Unlike fiber, GPUs depreciate in 3–6 years, so an overbuild does not leave a decades-long cheap asset; this weakens the "users inherit the glut" argument relative to 2002.
- The dynamo analogy (productivity lag of decades after electrification) is consistent with the Dallas Fed finding that productivity gains appear only in the most exposed 16% of hours so far.
