## KQ1. Application software / SaaS: who disclosed quantified AI revenue, did growth re-accelerate, and where did seats compress?

### Takeaway
Nearly every large SaaS vendor now reports an AI ARR/ACV line that is growing 150-240% YoY, but at the whole-company level growth has NOT re-accelerated: Salesforce (+11%), Workday (+13% sub), Intuit (+10%), Adobe (+13%), HubSpot (~20%), Monday (+22%) are flat-to-decelerating, and the market has de-rated them anyway (Salesforce -26-30%, Workday -33%, HubSpot -40-56%, Intuit -49%, Adobe -23-31%, Monday -38-44% YTD at various 2026 dates). AI ARR is real but small relative to the base (Salesforce Agentforce $1.5B on ~$46B revenue; Workday ~$600M on ~$10B subscription), and seat compression shows up mainly as (a) the vendors' own support/engineering headcount cuts and (b) explicit 10-K risk language (Workday) rather than as reported revenue declines; the one clear exception is Atlassian, whose FQ3 2026 (+32% revenue, Jira seat expansion) directly contradicted the seat-compression bear case.

### Inferences
- "AI ARR" disclosures are growing fast in percentage terms but are 3-6% of revenue at the large vendors and have not offset core deceleration; the market is pricing seat/pricing risk on the ~95% of revenue that is not AI, not rewarding the 5% that is.
- Definition changes (Salesforce folding Slackbot/Headless 360 into Agentforce; moving to milestone reporting) are a yellow flag that organic Agentforce growth may be slowing; treat Agentforce ARR growth rates as marketing-adjacent until reported on a constant definition.
- The most concrete "seat compression" evidence so far is vendors cutting their OWN seats (Salesforce support 9,000 -> 5,000; ServiceNow no-backfill; GitLab -14%), which is both a cost tailwind for them and a demonstration of the demand headwind they pose to each other.
- Pricing-model migration (ServiceNow 50% non-seat net-new; Workday Flex Credits; GitLab usage credits; Salesforce Agentic Work Units) is the main structural response; none has yet produced reported revenue re-acceleration.

## KQ2. Coding agents: productivity evidence, market share, run-rates, and effect on GitLab/Atlassian; is "software gets cheaper to build" visible?

### Takeaway
Coding is the one category where AI revenue is unambiguously large and measured: Cursor went from ~$1B to >$4B annualized revenue in ~7 months (Nov 2025 -> Jun 2026) and was acquired by SpaceX for ~$60B; Anthropic disclosed Claude Code at >$2.5B run-rate in Feb 2026 (company-wide run-rate reported at $47B by May and ~$65B by July 2026); GitHub Copilot's revenue grew >60% QoQ after moving to usage billing. Independent productivity evidence is far weaker than the revenue: METR's RCT found a 19% slowdown with early-2025 tools and its 2026 follow-up is statistically inconclusive, while telemetry vendors (Faros) show large individual-output gains with flat org-level delivery. The "cheaper to build" effect is visible in AI-native startup headcount (25-40% smaller teams; >$100M ARR with <100 staff) and in incumbents' own layoffs (GitLab -14%, Block -40%), not yet in public software gross margins.

### Inferences
- Revenue capture in coding is concentrated in model owners (Anthropic, OpenAI, Microsoft/GitHub) and one app-layer winner (Cursor, now inside SpaceX); the gap between measured productivity (ambiguous) and willingness to pay (enormous) suggests buyers are paying for perceived speed and output volume, which is consistent with METR's "perception exceeds measurement" finding.
- GitLab is the clearest public "legacy dev tool" test: growth ~20% with 117% NRR is holding, but it is cutting staff and adding usage pricing, i.e., defending with cost and model change rather than showing AI-driven acceleration.
- Lower cost to build shows up in startup org charts and incumbent layoffs; public software gross margins are if anything being pressured by AI inference costs (Snowflake, Databricks in KQ5), so "cheaper software" is a cost-of-labor story, not a cost-of-goods story yet.

## KQ3. IT services: AI deflation of billing, headcount, bookings, stock performance; winners vs losers

### Takeaway
Measured deflation is now explicit: TCS says ~50% of active engagements have been AI-repriced with contract values down 10-20% on average, Infosys cut FY27 guidance twice (to 1-2% CC growth) citing pricing, and Cognizant's Q2 bookings fell 6% even as fixed-price/outcome contracts passed 50% of revenue. Accenture (FY26 revenue +5% LC, record $84B+ bookings, stock +20% on the Oct 1 print) and Capgemini (H1 2026 +11.3% CC, "pricing stable") are the relative winners; EPAM (guide cut, AI-native revenue 11% but big deals slipping to 2027), Cognizant (-36% YTD), Infosys and Wipro (0.6% growth) are the losers. AI implementation revenue is real (Infosys AI = 10.5% of revenue; EPAM >$160M/qtr; Accenture 400+ advanced-AI clients) but is being more than offset by deflation on the legacy book.

### Inferences
- Priced in: a permanent step-down in seat-based SaaS growth and pricing power (median ~4.5x NTM revenue is near 2022-23 trough levels), and continued hypergrowth at consumption/AI leaders (20-40x). Not priced in: evidence that AI ARR at the incumbents can re-accelerate total growth (any such print, like Atlassian's FQ3, produces 20-30% single-day moves).
- The market's dividing line matches the measured one: consumption-priced data/infra and security re-accelerated and re-rated; seat-priced application vendors decelerated and de-rated; IT services and BPO de-rated on explicit billing deflation. The "SaaSpocalypse" narrative overstates the index-level damage (IGV ~flat YTD by late Sep) but understates the single-name damage in seat-based names (-40-56%).
